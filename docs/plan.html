Nigerian Language ASR Benchmark

A research and delivery plan for measuring speech recognition quality in Hausa, Yorùbá, Igbo and Nigerian Pidgin

Author: Musa Musa Contact: musabrillz@gmail.com Date: 7 August 2026 Version: 1.4 Status: Plan — not yet started

Executive summary

Public ASR leaderboards score models on clean, read speech. Real Nigerian deployments are narrowband telephone audio, ambient noise, and constant code-switching — conditions no existing benchmark measures. This work measures Nigerian-language ASR under those conditions instead.

It is deliberately not a machine-learning project: no model training, no fine-tuning, no novel architecture. It is an evaluation harness that calls existing public models and computes standard error metrics — ordinary backend engineering applied to a question the field has left open.

Deliverables: an open-source harness (MIT), degradation curves across four models, four languages and seven acoustic conditions with an English control, entity- and numeral-level error rates for Nigerian languages, the first measurement of whether an LLM correction stage helps or harms them, and a write-up targeting AfricaNLP or LREC. Cost: USD 60–130 in GPU and API time. Effort: eight weekends plus one evening.

The two contributions

1. The tone-orthography experiment. Yorùbá, Igbo and Hausa are all tonal but differ in whether tone is written (§2.2). Under one protocol, does orthographic tone marking predict ASR quality independently of training-data volume? No prior work compares Nigerian languages this way.

2. The LLM-repair asymmetry. Production pipelines are no longer ASR alone — they are ASR followed by LLM error correction, which works well in English. For Nigerian languages an LLM may replace a garbled name or numeral with a fluent, plausible, wrong one: output fluency rises, WER may even improve, and entity accuracy falls. WER cannot detect this by construction. Measuring it requires entity-level scoring (§5.1) and has not been done for African languages.

Evidential status — read before citing

Established elsewhere, not here. That low-resource ASR degrades sharply under noise is a known result — a Sundanese/Javanese study reports 2–3× WER degradation at low SNR, and Indic-language work finds narrowband (2G GSM) bandwidth limitation to be the single most critical audio factor. This project tests whether that pattern holds for Nigerian languages and whether tone-marking modulates it. That is replication-and-extension in a new setting, not a new hypothesis — and it is the stronger position, because it supplies a predicted effect size to test against.

Out of scope. No public spontaneous Nigerian-language speech exists, so the read→spontaneous gap cannot be measured here. Acoustic degradation is a measurable proxy; spontaneity is not. Do not claim a spontaneity result. See §8 and Milestone 5.

1.3 The circularity problem

Why current numbers are misleading

Meta's MMS model achieved 1,000+ language coverage by training largely on recordings of the New Testament — the only large parallel audio corpus spanning that many languages. Meanwhile, the dominant public Hausa dataset is also religious reading.

So a model trained on religious audio is evaluated on religious audio, in a field where religious audio dominates what is available. Reported performance is therefore optimistic by an unknown margin — and no one has quantified it.

1.5 What remains open

After that correction, four things are genuinely unmeasured:

  • Nigerian languages under degradation. The robustness studies above cover Austronesian and Indic languages. No equivalent exists for Hausa, Yorùbá, Igbo or Nigerian Pidgin.
  • Tone-marking as a variable. No work compares languages that differ in whether tone is orthographically marked, under one protocol (§2.2).
  • Whether LLM correction helps or harms African-language ASR. Asked once, for one European low-resource language. The mechanism that should make it fail — hallucination on entities outside the model's pretraining distribution — applies far more strongly to Nigerian names, places and numeral constructions.
  • Entity-level scoring for Nigerian languages. The metrics exist; they have not been applied here, and they are the only instruments that can detect a fluent-but-wrong substitution.
Strategic consequence

Do not position this as "the Nigerian ASR leaderboard." Microsoft Research has built that, with far greater resources. Position it as the question the leaderboards structurally cannot answer, because they score clean read speech only: how do these models behave under the conditions Nigerians actually use them in?

2. Language selection

2.1 Recommendation: build for four, lead with Hausa

An earlier version of this plan proposed Hausa only. That was the wrong scope. The harness is language-agnostic, and both target datasets (FLEURS and Common Voice) already contain Hausa, Yorùbá and Igbo. Covering all three costs no additional engineering — the same harness, normaliser and scoring code serve every language. It does multiply GPU time roughly fourfold, which is the bulk of the ~40 GPU-hours and the USD 60–130 in §4; that is a compute cost, not a design cost, and it is the main reason the budget is what it is.

More importantly, the cross-language comparison is the intellectual contribution. "Hausa ASR is poor" is an observation. "Yorùbá scores four times better than Hausa under identical evaluation conditions, and here is the evidence for why" is a research finding.

2.2 The natural experiment

The three languages differ systematically in how tone is written, which makes them an unusually clean comparison:

  • Yorùbá — tonal, and tone is marked orthographically with diacritics. Best measured performance.
  • Igbo — tonal, tone usually not marked in standard writing. Middling performance.
  • Hausa — tonal, tone not marked in standard orthography. Worst performance.

This yields a testable question: does orthographic tone marking correlate with ASR quality, independently of training-data volume? If it does, that has direct implications for how low-resource speech data should be transcribed — a finding useful well beyond Nigeria.

The three languages also stress text normalisation differently (hooked letters vs. subdot letters vs. tone diacritics), which makes the normalisation analysis richer across four languages than one.

2.3 Why Hausa still leads

CriterionRationale
Largest populationBiggest L1 base of the three, plus substantial L2 use across five countries

Highest need for voiceNorthern Nigeria has lower literacy rates, so voice is the primary viable interface rather than a convenience
Least researcher attentionYorùbá already has a decade-spanning NLP survey literature; Hausa has markedly less

Nigerian Pidgin should be included as a fourth if data permits. With well over 100 million L2 speakers it is arguably the most widely understood language in the country, and because it is English-lexified, model behaviour on it is genuinely unpredictable — models may perform unexpectedly well, or fail in unexpected ways. Either outcome is interesting.

3. Use cases — why the measurement matters

3.1 Error rate is the viability gate

Word error rate is not an academic score; it determines what can be built. Rough thresholds:

WERWhat becomes possible
≤ 10%Usable transcripts; captioning, documentation, archive search
10–25%Transcripts with human correction; reliable intent detection and call routing
25–40%Not usable as text, but often sufficient to classify what the caller wants
> 50%Broadly unusable

A service designer choosing between a 25% and a 65% error rate is choosing between a viable product and an impossible one. At present they cannot make that choice on evidence, because the evidence does not exist.

These thresholds are necessary but not sufficient

WER thresholds describe prose quality. They say nothing about the words that carry the payload — a dosage, an age, a place name, a quantity. A transcript at 20% WER whose every numeral is wrong is worse than useless in a clinical setting, and WER reports it as good. Sector viability must therefore be judged on entity-level accuracy (§5.1), not on WER alone.

The one cited figure (70–77% medication-slot accuracy being clinically insufficient) comes from the medical ASR literature. Establishing real per-sector thresholds for Nigerian languages would require deployment studies that do not yet exist.

3.3 The delivery channel

In practice the interface is not a bespoke app. It is WhatsApp voice notes and IVR phone lines — the channels already in universal use. Any downstream product built on this benchmark should assume that pipeline: voice note in, transcript, intent, spoken reply out.

Sequencing rules

Milestone 0 is where projects die. Unfamiliar ecosystem, and everything fails for boring reasons — wrong config name, gated dataset, missing audio codec. Do nothing else that evening.

Do not rent a GPU before Milestone 3. Everything prior runs on a laptop with tiny models. Never debug a harness and a cloud bill at the same time.

Budget headroom for reruns. The ~40 GPU-hour figure assumes everything works first time. It will not. Assume at least one full re-run after a bug is found in the normaliser or the degradation pipeline, and price accordingly.

5. Methodology

5.1 Metrics

Word Error Rate — edit distance at word level, normalised by reference length:

WER = (Substitutions + Deletions + Insertions) / Reference_word_count

Character Error Rate — the same formula at character level. Reported alongside WER because it distinguishes "wrong word" from "nearly-right word", which matters for morphologically rich languages.

Entity Error Rate and Numeral Error Rate — the same alignment, filtered to the tokens that carry meaning: numbers, dates, quantities, personal and place names. Scored strictly, so any error within a multi-token entity fails the whole entity.

Why entities are scored separately

These are established metrics, not a contribution of this work — entity- and slot-level ASR evaluation dates to Interspeech 2015, and medical ASR already uses MC-WER for the same reason. WER weights every word equally, so substituting one medication for another scores exactly as badly as substituting "the" for "a". In clinical evaluation, systems reaching 70–77% medication-slot accuracy are documented as insufficient for autonomous use while showing acceptable WER.

Nigerian languages add two stressors: numerals are where code-switching concentrates — speakers routinely state amounts and dates in English mid-sentence — and Yorùbá numerals are structurally complex (vigesimal and subtractive). Both are unmeasured here.

5.2 The LLM-correction arm

Production pipelines now append an LLM generative-error-correction (GER) stage to ASR. Every transcript is therefore scored twice: raw ASR output, and the same output after LLM correction.

The failure this is designed to catch

LLMs are documented as prone to hallucination when resolving rare named entities outside their pretraining distribution. For Hausa, Yorùbá and Igbo, essentially every personal name, place name and numeral construction is outside that distribution. The predicted failure is not that correction does nothing — it is that the LLM replaces a garbled entity with a fluent, plausible, wrong one.

When that happens, WER can improve while entity accuracy falls. Reporting WER alone would record this as a success. The paired WER / entity-accuracy comparison across the two arms is the measurement.

Scoping. The correction arm applies to a bounded subset — two ASR models × four languages × three conditions (C0, C2, C4) on a fixed 200-utterance sample — not the full grid. ASR inference is not repeated; only the correction call and re-scoring.

Contamination control. An LLM may have memorised the evaluation transcripts, inflating apparent correction quality. FLEURS derives from FLoRes, which is public and well-indexed, so this is a live risk. Follow the contamination-aware protocol from the West Frisian study (§1.4) and report the check explicitly.

5.3 Normalisation — the credibility-critical decision

Consider reference ɗaya against hypothesis daya. The model heard the word correctly but wrote plain d for hooked ɗ.

  • Raw WER: 100% on that word — different strings, counted as a substitution.
  • Normalised WER: 0% — after mapping ɗ → d.

Both are correct answers to different questions. Did the model recognise the word? — normalised. Can this output be used directly in a Hausa application? — raw, because wrong orthography is wrong.

Rule

Publish both figures, with the normalisation function visible in the repository. Reporting only normalised overstates usability; reporting only raw measures orthographic convention rather than recognition. Publishing both, transparently, is what distinguishes a benchmark from an opinion.

Required external validation

Orthographic decisions — hooked-letter handling, diacritic stripping, tone-mark treatment — must be reviewed by native speakers of each language before publication. This is not optional polish. If these calls are wrong, every number in the paper is wrong, and the first native-speaking reader will say so publicly.

5.4 Evaluation conditions and the English control

Both target datasets contain read speech, so comparing them alone measures recording-condition variation, not the read→spontaneous gap. To obtain a genuine degradation curve, audio is systematically degraded using standard techniques rather than sourced from new recordings:

ConditionHow producedWhat it tests
C0 — cleanFLEURS as distributed (16 kHz)Baseline; comparable to published figures
C1 — varied recordingCommon Voice as distributedReal microphone, room and speaker variation
C2 — narrowbandFLEURS resampled 16 k → 8 k → 16 kTelephone channel (IVR, voice calls)
C3 — noisyFLEURS + additive noise at 20 / 10 / 5 dB SNRAmbient noise (market, street, clinic)
C4 — narrowband + noiseC2 and C3 combinedRealistic deployment worst case
The control is what makes the claim testable

Run the identical protocol on FLEURS English — the six FLEURS-derived conditions (C0, C2, C3 at three SNRs, C4). C1 is excluded from the control because it is a different corpus, not a degradation, and no comparable English arm exists. Four models × six conditions = the 24 control runs in Milestone 3.

Without an English baseline, "Nigerian languages degrade badly" has nothing to be worse than — it is an assertion. With it, the comparison becomes a measurement.

Report both absolute WER increase and the relative degradation ratio (WERcondition ÷ WERclean). A language already at 60% clean WER has little headroom left, so absolute figures alone can hide a floor effect and make a badly-degrading language look robust.

5.5 Statistical treatment

Bootstrap resampling over utterances to produce 95% confidence intervals on each WER figure. Without intervals, a model ranking is not evidence — reviewers will say so, correctly.

5.6 Error analysis

Sample ~100 errors per language and categorise by hand: tone confusions, hooked-letter or diacritic substitutions, English loanwords, code-switching, morphological affixes, hallucinated insertions. Report the distribution.

This is roughly one day of work and it is the difference between a table and a contribution. It is also the part that tells a practitioner whether a model's failures are systematic and fixable, or diffuse and fatal.

6.1 Practical frictions for an independent researcher

  • arXiv endorsement — first-time cs.CL submitters generally need endorsement from an established author. Resolve early, not the night before a deadline.
  • Registration fees — acceptance at a major venue usually requires paid registration. Waivers and virtual attendance are frequently available for researchers from under-represented regions; ask.
  • No institutional review — a co-author is the substitute for the feedback an institution would otherwise provide.
Strong recommendation

Find a co-author — Masakhane, or a Nigerian university speech/NLP group. It solves arXiv endorsement, supplies native-speaker validation, and provides pre-review. It also changes the character of the work: a collaboration with speakers of the languages is a stronger position than a solo outsider benchmarking someone else's language. Approach them after Milestone 1, when there is a working harness to show.

7. Funding path

The benchmark itself is not fundable — it costs USD 60–130. What it unlocks is: a purpose-built evaluation corpus of spontaneous, telephone-quality Nigerian-language speech, which is genuinely expensive (speaker recruitment, consent, transcription, quality control) and genuinely valuable.

Sequence: self-fund the benchmark → publish → the results become the evidence base for the grant application. "I believe Hausa ASR is poor" is weak. "Here is my published measurement showing degradation from 25% to 65% WER across domains, here is my working harness, here is the corpus I propose to build" is a serious proposal.

9. Decision point

Milestone 4 is the natural stopping point — publish, then assess response before committing further. Natural extensions if it lands: additional languages, the spoken-quantities set (Milestone 5), and the spontaneous-speech corpus. A hosted leaderboard is not on that list — PazaBench already occupies it (§1.4), and building one would abandon the positioning this plan depends on.

If it does not land, the floor is still a public artifact, a working harness, and genuine fluency in a domain that was unfamiliar a month earlier.

Do not plan past Milestone 4 yet. Milestone 3b sits before it and is in scope; Milestone 5 is deferred until after publication.
